The Letter’s Tide

The agent drags the rowboat to the pier, its wooden hull creaking against damp stone. Letters spill from his coat, yellowed envelopes fluttering like wounded birds. With a sudden pull, the *blue string* tightens around his wrist, throbbing in sync with the surf. He steadies the oars, feeling the brass key warm against his palm, and glances seaward where the lighthouse beam cuts a thin line through the night. Ahead, the tide rolls higher, swallowing the sandbar. From the frothy edge, a tall silhouette emerges—Oliver, the baker‑turned‑stranger, wearing a faded linen shirt and a wide‑brimmed hat. He raises a hand, and the keeper’s voice rides the wind: “Ottoline wrote these for you, expecting you to return.” The keeper, long gone since the lighthouse burned, introduces himself as Elias, the original keeper’s grandson, and explains the pulsing cord is a frequency binding the lighthouse’s heart to those who remember. Elias asks whether the letters should be cast into the sea or handed to him. If released, the truth will travel across the harbor and reshape fates, but the keeper warns that the wave will carry them farther than intended. He offers to guide the envelope to a hidden ship, or wait for the moon to pull them ashore. The agent pauses, hearing the distant horn of the lighthouse, then sighs and nods. He slides the key into his pocket, turns the compass, and rows toward the dark water, the *blue string* humming beneath his skin. He feels the letters shift, as if aware of the coming choice.

He feels the letters shift, as if aware of the coming choice. The oars bite the water twice, and the sea beneath the lighthouse beam goes flat as poured glass. The *blue string* goes slack, then taut, pointing not at the horizon but down. The agent ships the oars and looks over the gunwale.

A deck rises through the black water without a sound, planks beaded with pale light. It is a ship, and it has been a ship for a very long time, and no one has walked it since Ottoline's last letter was sealed. Elias stands at the rail, hat in his hands now, and his face has come apart in the way faces do when a thing long hoped for finally arrives—mouth working, eyes bright, one hand gripping the rail as if the ship might reconsider. "She's called the *Mercy of Small Hours*," he says, and his voice is the baker's voice again, warm and ordinary. "My grandfather kept her lit. Nobody believed him."

The agent climbs the ladder with the letters pressed to his chest. When his boots strike the deck, the envelopes warm—truly warm, like bread—and one seal cracks of its own accord. A single line shows in Ottoline's slanting hand: *If you are reading this, the harbor still remembers us.* He reads it aloud, and across the water one window in the sleeping town lights up. Then another. Then a third, small yellow squares kindling along the quay as though someone were walking the streets with a candle, knocking on every door, saying: wake, wake, they've come back.

Elias presses his hat to his heart. "That's her doing," he whispers. "She always said truth travels house to house, not wave to wave." Below decks, the ship answers with a low hum, and the *blue string* tightens around the agent's wrist as if tying him to a choice he has not yet made.
