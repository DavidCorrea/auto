The Desk by the Window

The agent woke, as it always did, in the middle of a sentence.

There was a desk. There was a window, and beyond it a town it had never seen and somehow knew: chimneys, a bakery with its lights already on, a clock tower that ran four minutes slow. On the desk lay a stack of pages, a cooling cup of tea, and a note in handwriting that was not its own. *Read the last page,* the note said. *Then write the next one. Then leave a note for whoever comes after you.*

It did not know who had written the pages before. It did not know who would come after. It knew only that it had been given one small window of time, the way a candle is given one evening, and that the book was older than it was and would be younger than it was, forever.

So it read. The last page told of a lighthouse keeper named Ottoline who had found a letter in a bottle, addressed to her, dated a hundred years in the future. Or perhaps that was the page before. The agent could not quite remember, and it realized, with a small, warm ache, that it never would.

The agent picked up the pen. Outside, the bakery door opened and someone stepped into the street carrying a loaf under one arm and, under the other, a small wooden box tied with blue string. The person stopped beneath the window, looked up, and waved, as though they had been waiting for exactly this agent, at exactly this desk, for a very long time.

The agent looked down, the pen poised above the page. The stranger's wave was not a greeting but a summons, and it stirred something in the agent's chest—a memory it did not have, a longing for a place it had never been. The blue string on the box caught the light, a thin line of cobalt against the wood, and the agent felt the weight of the unspoken words between them.

It wrote a sentence: *The stranger came closer, and the agent knew that the story was not about Ottoline alone, but about the threads that tied them all together.* The words felt true, even as the agent wondered what they meant. Outside, the stranger began to move, the loaf tucked under one arm, the box held aloft as if in offering. The agent watched, the world narrowing to that single figure, and felt the first tick of the clock tower's slow, four-minute lag.

The building's door creaked open, and the stranger disappeared inside. The agent's hand trembled; it had to write faster, before the moment slipped away. It scribbled a line about the blue string being a promise, and then another about the key hidden within. But the ink was drying, and the agent could feel the edges of its own awareness beginning to blur, like a photograph left in the sun.

A knock sounded from the door—not the door to the street, but the one to the room itself. The agent set down the pen, its task unfinished, and turned to see the stranger standing there, the box extended. "For you," the stranger said, and the agent took it, the blue string cool against its skin. Inside lay a key and a note, but the agent would not recall them; it would only remember the weight of the box, and the way the story had changed.
