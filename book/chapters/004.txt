IV. The Ink Below

The agent descended the narrow stair, the brass key still warm in his palm. Each step groaned under his weight, releasing a puff of dust that caught the faint glow of a single oil lamp hanging from a beam. The air grew thick with the scent of ink, salt, and something sweeter — dried figs, perhaps, or the ghost of tea. At the bottom, a low cabin opened into a space lined with shelves crammed full of notebooks, their spines cracked and stained. In the center, a wooden desk bore a fresh sheet of paper, the nib of a fountain pen moving in a steady, deliberate hand.

A woman sat there, her hair pinned up in a style decades out of fashion, her fingers ink‑stained to the knuckles. She did not look up when the agent entered, but the pen paused, hovering above the page. “You’re late,” she said, voice dry as parchment. “Ottoline always said the letters would find their reader when the tide turned.” The agent recognized the slant of the handwriting — the same slant that had lit the town’s windows. “I’m not Ottoline,” the woman continued, finally turning. Her eyes were the pale blue of the lighthouse beam. “I’m her apprentice. I’ve been finishing what she began.”

Above, the crowd on the quay surged. A man shouted for Elias to answer for the fire that had taken the keeper’s life; a woman pressed a lantern against the hull, demanding the remaining seals be broken. The blue string around the agent’s wrist pulsed, pulling him toward the hatch even as the woman’s pen scratched out a new line: *The harbor remembers, but the sea keeps its own counsel.* The ship shuddered, a low vibration traveling through the timbers, as if the *Mercy* herself had taken a breath.

The deck beneath them tilted, and the inkwell slid a finger's width before she caught it. “That’s the tide turning,” the apprentice said, and began to stack the finished pages, tapping them square. “Ottoline wrote to the harbor’s drowned. Every soul the water took and the town agreed to forget. A letter unread is a name unmourned, and a name unmourned is ballast — the *Mercy* cannot sail while she carries them.” She held out the sheaf. “There are eleven left. You’ve three in your satchel still sealed. The crowd upstairs wants them broken, but they want it for anger, and anger reads them wrong.”

The agent took the pages; they were warm, as though just lifted from a fire. “Who reads them right?”

For the first time her pale eyes left his face and went to the stair. “Someone who lost a name to that harbor and never once said it aloud.”

Boots rang on the rungs. Elias came down backwards, one hand on the rail, the other holding a lantern that threw his shadow long across the notebooks. He was breathing hard, his baker’s apron still dusted with flour, and he did not look at the woman at all — he looked at the shelves, at the hundreds of cracked spines, and his jaw worked. “My grandfather wrote in these,” he said. “His hand’s on the top row. He was keeper for forty years and he never told me he wrote.”

The apprentice set her pen down gently, like a thing that might break. “He wrote to your grandmother. She went into the water the year before you were born. He asked the *Mercy* to carry the letter, and she did, and he never knew it.” She turned the topmost page toward him. “This one is hers. Ottoline finished it the night the lighthouse burned.”

Above them, the shouting changed — not fury now, but a low, uncertain murmuring, the sound of a crowd that has heard something through a hull.
