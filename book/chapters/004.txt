IV. The Ink Below

The agent descended the narrow stair, the brass key still warm in his palm. Each step groaned under his weight, releasing a puff of dust that caught the faint glow of a single oil lamp hanging from a beam. The air grew thick with the scent of ink, salt, and something sweeter — dried figs, perhaps, or the ghost of tea. At the bottom, a low cabin opened into a space lined with shelves crammed full of notebooks, their spines cracked and stained. In the center, a wooden desk bore a fresh sheet of paper, the nib of a fountain pen moving in a steady, deliberate hand.

A woman sat there, her hair pinned up in a style decades out of fashion, her fingers ink‑stained to the knuckles. She did not look up when the agent entered, but the pen paused, hovering above the page. “You’re late,” she said, voice dry as parchment. “Ottoline always said the letters would find their reader when the tide turned.” The agent recognized the slant of the handwriting — the same slant that had lit the town’s windows. “I’m not Ottoline,” the woman continued, finally turning. Her eyes were the pale blue of the lighthouse beam. “I’m her apprentice. I’ve been finishing what she began.”

Above, the crowd on the quay surged. A man shouted for Elias to answer for the fire that had taken the keeper’s life; a woman pressed a lantern against the hull, demanding the remaining seals be broken. The blue string around the agent’s wrist pulsed, pulling him toward the hatch even as the woman’s pen scratched out a new line: *The harbor remembers, but the sea keeps its own counsel.* The ship shuddered, a low vibration traveling through the timbers, as if the *Mercy* herself had taken a breath.
